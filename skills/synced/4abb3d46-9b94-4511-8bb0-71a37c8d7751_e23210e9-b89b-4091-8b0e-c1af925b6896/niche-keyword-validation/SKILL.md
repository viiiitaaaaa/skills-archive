# Niche & Keyword Validation

Growth percentage is the least reliable number on the screen. Sorting by it surfaces exactly the keywords that should be discarded, because percentage and volume are inversely related in this data: three- and four-digit growth appears almost exclusively where the base is near zero.

Expect a pass rate around 2-3%. In one working session, roughly twenty categories were screened and one survived. That is the normal result — the purpose of the exercise is to discard.

## Order of checks

Cheapest first, because each stage kills most of what reaches it.

1. **Fix the phrasing** — planner, find the wording that carries volume
2. **Google Trends, free** — brands, interest level, intent, five-year trajectory
3. **Keyword Planner, costs a lookup** — competition and absolute volume

Never skip to step 3 on an unvalidated keyword, and never stop at step 2 on a keyword you intend to bet on.

## Step 1 — Find the phrasing that carries volume

Trend tools rank by growth, not volume, so the phrase they display is often a minor variant of a much larger keyword that never appears in the interface at all. Look the keyword up in a planner and scan related phrasings before spending anything else.

The gap is routinely enormous: a trend tool surfaced `lamp warmer` at 59% growth — 1,300 searches/month. The phrase people actually use is `candle lamp warmer` — 110,000/month, 85× larger, absent from the trend tool's list entirely.

**Phrasing beats category.** Across many niches, specific action- or audience-based phrasings sustain real growth while the umbrella term stays flat:

| Works | Doesn't |
|---|---|
| compression socks for flying | travel compression socks |
| compression socks for nurses | compression socks for standing all day |
| litter trapping mat | cat litter mat |
| rake brush | brush for dogs |
| shaver for bald head | bald head shaver |

Note the nurses/standing-all-day pair — "nurse" is an identity, "stands all day" is merely a condition, and only the identity has volume. People search by what they're doing or who they are.

Two limits on this. Phrasing can be so specific that nobody uses it (`pet hair remover for couch` returned two queries total). And a pain only works as a phrasing if the audience actually articulates it — sock wearers never search "socks that stay on," they just search brands known not to slip.

## Step 2 — Google Trends, free

This closes almost everything. Set period to five years where trajectory matters, one year where composition matters, and pick the target country. Download the CSV — it carries a `search interest` column that instantly separates real volume from noise.

**Read the composition of the left column first.** If the top is descriptive and free of manufacturer names, the niche is open. If PetKit, Dyson, Philips, Starface, or Nike sit at the top, close it and move on. This single read resolves most candidates in seconds.

**Then read the trajectory.** Trend tools draw a smoothed line over the noisy monthly series; that smoothed line is the only reliable signal. Accept monotonic rise across the full period. Reject flat-for-years-then-recent-spike — that's a campaign or an artifact.

**Then read intent** (see below), then geography (one region at 100 with the rest blank means the sample is statistically empty), then whether the related-trends panel is populated at all.

## Interest is not purchase intent

A rising line measures attention. It cannot distinguish someone about to buy from someone looking for a way to avoid buying. Read the query composition to tell them apart.

**Non-commercial** — DIY, cardboard, homemade, cheap, easy, ideas, at home, how to make, plus advice sources (Reddit, university extension, nonprofits). `cat enrichment` showed 6,600/month with excellent trend-tool growth and a query list saturated with cardboard box, toilet paper roll, and egg carton. The intent was explicitly "entertain my cat without spending money."

**Commercial** — best, what type, what level, do I need, comparisons, sizes and grades. The buyer has accepted they'll purchase and is choosing.

**Skepticism** — does it work, is it legit, evidence, side effects, heavy Reddit and Consumer Reports reliance. The category itself is under suspicion; the store must sell proof before product. Very hard for a new brand.

**Retail capture** — a long tail of store names (Walmart, Boots, Argos, CVS, Costco, Chemist Warehouse) plus `near me`. Buyers know what they want and are choosing where to collect it. An unknown independent isn't in that consideration set.

**Price race** — when growing queries are dominated by marketplaces and fast fashion (SHEIN, AliExpress) rather than product attributes, the category is already competing on price alone. Contrast with material qualifiers below.

This check matters most for exactly the keywords a trend tool ranks highest, since novelty attracts curiosity before it attracts purchases.

## Signals that reveal a niche's stage

**Material qualifiers are the strongest positive signal available.** When growing queries specify a material — steel, stainless, hydrocolloid, silicone, wooden, merino — buyers understand the properties and will pay for the better one. Repeatedly observed: `stainless steel gua sha` +190% while `gua sha stone` −30%; `hydrocolloid patches` +120% inside a brand-saturated category; `stainless steel litter box` +110% across three separate slices. The material qualifier is often the only open door in an otherwise occupied category.

**Falling "best" queries mean the market has closed.** Rising "best" = buyers seeking advice, interceptable with content. Falling "best" alongside rising brand queries = the choice has been made for them. In one litter-box slice, six separate "best" variants fell while seven brands rose, and `automatic litter box reddit` fell 30% — nobody asks for advice anymore.

**A falling former leader with rising challengers** means the category has entered price competition. `litter robot 4` −30% while Catlink, PetKit, Neakasa and Meowant all rose.

**Verdicts don't transfer between synonyms.** Within one category, `automatic` was fully brand-occupied while `self cleaning` was not. Test each phrasing separately.

## Step 3 — Keyword Planner, spend the lookup

Trend tools cannot measure competition at all. The planner's competition column answers a question nothing else does, and it is the fastest disqualifier available: **at the top of the scale (100 / "High"), stop — no growth figure rescues an occupied niche.** Six consecutive candidates died on this column across hair devices, grooming, styling tools, cat products, cameras and home fragrance.

Planner exports are frequently returned with placeholder values — every cell reading 88,888 or 8.88 means the account lacks data access. The keyword list itself remains fully usable and is often more diagnostic than the numbers would have been, because query composition reveals intent, brands, retail capture and regulatory exposure.

Occasionally a real figure survives among placeholders and explains everything: a single `+2,994%` on a dropship brand name revealed the campaign that had lifted three separate keyword clusters and made them all look like emerging trends.

## Where the open niches actually are

Large brands don't enter categories where marketing can't be amortized over the price. Anything with a motor, battery or app is occupied by the time you arrive; the accessories around it usually aren't.

Confirmed repeatedly: automatic litter boxes held by seven brands while the mat under them had none; hair styling tools owned by Dyson while heatless curl accessories stayed open; intimate shavers owned by Philips and Gillette while post-shave care stayed open; gua sha creams entered by Korean beauty brands while the steel tool itself remained unclaimed.

This is a guide to where to look, not a guarantee — of eight cheap non-electronic candidates screened in one pass, zero survived. Cheap and simple is necessary, not sufficient.

## Building the funnel, not the product

A store is a set of positions serving one ritual or scenario, not a single item. Structure findings as: **anchor** (carries traffic and advertising), **add-ons** (raise basket value), **consumables** (drive repeat purchase), **content** (problem-phrased queries that rarely have product-page volume but rank easily and feed the anchor).

Check funnel depth before committing. A confirmed anchor with nothing behind it is a weak niche: one cat-mat anchor scored 26 interest while every proposed add-on — bowls, scoops, containers, stands — came in under 10, which collapses the funnel to three or four real positions.

## Reporting

Keep verdicts short: volume, trajectory shape, verdict, one line each. Every reply should end by naming the next 2-3 keywords worth checking and why, ordered by what would most change the decision — the user is spending metered lookups.

Say the low pass rate out loud early, so a long run of rejections reads as the method working rather than as failure.

**When new evidence overturns an earlier recommendation, say so plainly and name the signal that was missing.** This happens routinely because checks run in increasing order of cost and later checks see what earlier ones cannot. A recommendation built on a trend line and demolished by a competition score or a DIY-saturated query list wasn't a reasoning error — it was reasoning on incomplete evidence — but the user needs to see the correction, not a quietly revised conclusion.

## Deliverables

Spreadsheets suit this work: one sheet listing everything with filters, one ranking niches, one per niche. Score niches on growth, openness, barrier to entry and unit economics, and make the score a live formula so weights can be adjusted. Label openness honestly as an estimate — search volume measures demand, not competition.

Separate out keywords that grow but can't be stocked: services ("dog grooming services"), category names ("pet retail", "pet department"), brand names. They belong in the semantic map, never in a purchase order.
